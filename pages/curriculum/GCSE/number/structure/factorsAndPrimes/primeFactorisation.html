  7. Prime factorisation

Before this page: recognise prime numbers, find factor pairs, and read repeated multiplication in index notation.

Break every factor until only primes remain

Every whole number greater than 1 is either prime or composite. A composite number has a factor other than 1 and itself, so it splits into a factor pair, and each composite factor splits again.

The tree ends when every branch ends at a prime factor, and the product of the branch ends is the number at the start:

A factor tree for 84

Start with 84

84 is composite, so it splits into a factor pair.

Different trees finish with the same primes

84 can begin as 12 × 7, 6 × 14 or 4 × 21:

12 × 7 → 2 × 2 × 3 × 7
6 × 14 → 2 × 2 × 3 × 7
4 × 21 → 2 × 2 × 3 × 7

The prime factorisation is unique

Every whole number greater than 1 has one prime factorisation, apart from the order of its factors: 2 × 2 × 3 × 7 and 7 × 3 × 2 × 2 are the same factorisation of 84, written in two orders.

Repeated division collects the same factors

Divide 756 by the smallest prime that divides exactly, then repeat with the quotient:

Repeated division of 756

Begin with the smallest prime factor

756 is even, so 2 is the first prime factor.

  1. Divide by the smallest prime that divides exactly.
  2. Repeat with the quotient, testing the same prime again before moving to the next.
  3. Stop when the quotient is 1; the divisors down the side are all the prime factors.

Collect repeated primes in index form

756 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 7
↓ 756 = 2^2 × 3^3 × 7

An index of 1 is not written: the final 7 occurs once, so the answer ends in × 7, not × 7^1. The completed answer is in index form, with its prime bases in ascending order so that two factorisations can be compared prime by prime. “Write 756 as a product of its prime factors” asks for this form.

Multiply the prime powers to check

2^2 × 3^3 × 7 = 4 × 27 × 7 = 756. A lost 3 would give 2^2 × 3^2 × 7 = 252, not 756.

Factorise a chosen number

A factor tree can be built from any composite branch, and a branch can be split again or collapsed. The final leaves show the prime factorisation of the starting number.

Split any composite branch until every leaf is prime. A branch can be moved to another disc and collapsed again.

A whole number from 2 to 9,999 is needed.

360 has prime factors still to collect.

Common mistakes

  • Stopping at a composite factor Writing 90 = 9 × 10 leaves 9 and 10 unsplit, so it is a factor pair rather than a prime factorisation.
  • Losing a repeated factor Writing 72 = 2^2 × 3^2 gives 36; the complete factorisation is 2^3 × 3^2.
  • Using a composite base Writing 72 = 8 × 3^2 multiplies the three 2s out to 8, which is composite; the index form keeps them as 2^3.

Summary

  1. Split a composite number into factor pairs, or divide it repeatedly by primes.
  2. Continue until every remaining factor is prime, or the repeated-division quotient is 1.
  3. Every whole number greater than 1 has one prime factorisation, whichever tree or division reaches it.
  4. Keep every repeated copy of a prime factor.
  5. Write equal prime factors as powers, with prime bases in ascending order.
  6. Multiply the prime powers to recover the starting number and check the factorisation.
