  7. Prime factorisation

Before this page: recognise prime numbers, find factor pairs, and read repeated multiplication in index notation.

Break every factor until only primes remain

Every whole number greater than 1 is either prime or composite. A composite number has a factor other than 1 and itself: in 84 = 12 × 7, 7 is prime, but 12 splits again.

The splitting stops only when every branch ends at a prime factor. For 84, the branch ends are 2, 2, 3 and 7, so the product is 84 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 7.

A factor tree for 84

Start with 84

84 is composite, so it has a factor pair other than 1 and 84.

A prime factorisation is a product containing prime factors only

  1. 84 = 12 × 7 is a factor pair, but 12 is composite, so the factorisation is unfinished.
  2. 84 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 7 contains primes only, so it is complete.

Different trees finish with the same primes

84 can begin as 12 × 7, 6 × 14 or 4 × 21. Each start rearranges the branches, but every complete tree ends with two 2s, one 3 and one 7.

12 × 7 → 2 × 2 × 3 × 7
6 × 14 → 2 × 2 × 3 × 7
4 × 21 → 2 × 2 × 3 × 7

The prime factorisation is unique

Every whole number greater than 1 has exactly one prime factorisation, apart from the order of its factors. 2 × 2 × 3 × 7 and 7 × 3 × 2 × 2 are the same factorisation of 84, written in two orders.

Repeated division collects the same factors

Repeated division starts with the smallest prime that divides exactly, and tests that prime again before moving to a larger one. For 756 the divisors are 2, 2, 3, 3, 3 and 7, and the last quotient is 1.

Repeated division of 756

Begin with the smallest prime factor

756 is even, so 2 is the first prime factor.

  1. Divide by the smallest prime factor that divides exactly.
  2. Repeat with the quotient, testing the same prime again before moving to the next.
  3. Stop when the quotient is 1; the divisors down the side are all the prime factors.

Collect repeated primes in index form

The repeated factors in 756 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 7 are collected as powers. Two factors of 2 make 2^2; three factors of 3 make 3^3.

756 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 7
↓ 756 = 2^2 × 3^3 × 7

An index of 1 is not written: the final 7 occurs once, so the answer ends in × 7, not × 7^1. The completed answer is in index form, with its prime bases in ascending order so that two factorisations can be compared prime by prime.

Multiply the prime powers to check

2^2 × 3^3 × 7 = 4 × 27 × 7 = 756. Recovering the starting number checks that no branch or division has been lost.

Finish at primes, then count every copy

  • Stopping at a composite factor Writing 84 = 12 × 7 leaves 12 unsplit, so it is a factor pair rather than a prime factorisation.
  • Losing a repeated factor Writing 72 = 2^2 × 3^2 gives 36; the complete factorisation is 2^3 × 3^2.
  • Using a composite base Writing 72 = 8 × 3^2 multiplies the three 2s out to 8, which is composite; the index form keeps them as 2^3.

Summary

  1. Split a composite number into factor pairs, or divide it repeatedly by primes.
  2. Continue until every remaining factor is prime, or the repeated-division quotient is 1.
  3. Keep every repeated copy of a prime factor.
  4. Write equal prime factors as powers, with prime bases in ascending order.
  5. Multiply the prime powers to recover the starting number and check the factorisation.
