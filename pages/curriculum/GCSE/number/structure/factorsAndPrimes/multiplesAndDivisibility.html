Before this page: be able to list the factors of a number in pairs, and to divide and see what is left over from short division.

Counting in steps

Let us say that sausages come in packs of 6, and a pack cannot be split. One pack is 6, two packs are 12, and counting on in 6s gives 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, running on for as long as anyone keeps buying. Those are the multiples of 6, and they are the only numbers of sausages there are: there is no way to buy 25.

Whether a number belongs on the list comes down to a division. 24 ÷ 6 = 4 with nothing over, so 24 is four packs and a multiple of 6. 25 ÷ 6 = 4 r 1: four packs and one sausage that no pack supplies, which is why 25 cannot be bought, and the same goes for any number that leaves a remainder.

Where two counts meet

Let us also say that rolls come in packs of 4, and a barbecue wants the same number of rolls as sausages. Buying rolls reaches 4, 8, 12, 16 and onwards, and buying sausages reaches 6, 12, 18, 24 and onwards. Four rolls cannot be matched, because there is no way to buy 4 sausages, and 6 sausages cannot be matched with rolls. 12 can be bought both ways, three packs of rolls and two packs of sausages. This is an example of where you might use a number on both lists, called a common multiple of 4 and 6.

The common multiples of 4 and 6 up to 36

The numbers 1 to 36

Rolls are counted in 4s above the line and sausages in 6s below it.

From 12 onwards the two counts meet every 12: at 24, which is 12 × 2, and at 36, which is 12 × 3. Every later meeting is a multiple of the first, and 24 rolls with 24 sausages is the smallest order bought twice over.

That first meeting, 12, is the lowest common multiple of 4 and 6. For a pair as small as 4 and 6, listing the multiples finds it in a few steps; for numbers whose lists would run on for pages, it is found from their prime factors instead.

Testing a number without dividing it

Does 3 divide 4,932? Dividing it out answers that, but it is a long calculation for a yes or a no. Listing every factor of 4,932 asks the same question seventy times over: the next square number above it is 71 × 71 = 5,041, so nothing above 70 needs trying.

A divisibility test gives the yes or the no without the division. It reads the digits, and it says whether the remainder is zero, and that is all it says. 4,932 ends in 2, so it is a multiple of 2 and 2 is one of its factors, but the quotient, 2,466, still has to come from dividing. GCSE asks for six tests: four that read the last digits, and two that add them.

Reading the last digits

Every counting number is a multiple of 10 with its last digit added on: 4,932 is 4,930 and 2. A multiple of 10 is a whole number of 2s, of 5s and of 10s, so for those three tests the 4,930 looks after itself and the 2 alone decides. A 4 needs the cut one place further left, because a ten is not a whole number of 4s and a hundred is: 100 = 4 × 25.

Divisible by 2

The last digit is 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8.

The last digit of 4,932 is 2. Yes: 4,932 ÷ 2 = 2,466

Why it works

Every ten is five 2s, so a whole number of tens can never leave anything over. In 4,932 that takes care of the 493 tens, and only the last digit is left to decide. It is a 2, which is one whole 2, so nothing is left over at all: 4,932 ÷ 2 = 2,466.

Change the number to 4,933 and those 493 tens are untouched. Only the last digit has moved, and 3 is one 2 with 1 left over, giving 4,933 ÷ 2 = 2,466 r 1. That is the whole test: 0, 2, 4, 6 and 8 are whole numbers of 2s, and the other five digits each leave 1 behind.

Divisible by 5

The last digit is 0 or 5.

The last digit of 4,932 is 2. No: 4,932 ÷ 5 = 986 r 2

Why it works

Ten is two 5s, so once again the tens look after themselves and only the last digit is left to decide. In 4,932 that digit is 2, which is smaller than 5, so the whole 2 stays behind as the remainder: 4,932 ÷ 5 = 986 r 2.

Of the ten digits, only 0 and 5 are whole numbers of 5s. 1, 2, 3 and 4 are too small to make a 5 at all, and 6, 7, 8 and 9 make one 5 with a little over. That is why every multiple of 5 ends in 0 or 5.

Divisible by 10

The last digit is 0.

The last digit of 4,932 is 2. No: 4,932 ÷ 10 = 493 r 2

Why it works

Every ten divides by 10 exactly, so only the last digit is ever in play, and every digit from 1 to 9 is smaller than 10. None of them has a 10 inside it to give up, so each one survives as the remainder. In 4,932 that is the 2, giving 4,932 ÷ 10 = 493 r 2, and only a 0 leaves nothing behind.

The same test can be reached from 10 = 2 × 5. A number on both the 2 list and the 5 list ends in one of 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 and also in 0 or 5, and 0 is the only digit that manages both.

Divisible by 4

The number made by the last two digits is a multiple of 4.

The last two digits of 4,932 make 32, which is 4 × 8. Yes: 4,932 ÷ 4 = 1,233

Why it works

A ten is not a whole number of 4s: 10 = 4 × 2 + 2, with 2 left over. That is why this test reads two digits where the test for 2 reads one. 518 ends in 8, and 8 is a multiple of 4, but 18 is not, and 518 ÷ 4 = 129 r 2.

Adding the digits

For 9 no cut helps. A ten is not a whole number of 9s, a hundred is not, and neither is a thousand. Each of them is one more than a multiple of 9, though, and that is enough: 4,000 is 4 × 999 + 4, 900 is 9 × 99 + 9 and 30 is 3 × 9 + 3, so every place hands back its own digit, and those digits are what the test adds.

Divisible by 9

The digits add to a multiple of 9.

Adding the digits of 4,932 gives 4 + 9 + 3 + 2 = 18, and 9 divides 18. Yes: 4,932 ÷ 9 = 548

Why it works

Everything but the digits comes to 4 × 999 + 9 × 99 + 3 × 9 = 4,914, which is 9 × 546 and needs no testing. The digits left behind add to 4 + 9 + 3 + 2 = 18 = 9 × 2, two more 9s, so 4,932 ÷ 9 = 548.

A digit sum that is still too big to recognise can be added again, for the same reason. 18 splits the same way, its ten leaving 1 and its 8 leaving 8, so 18 and 1 + 8 = 9 leave the same remainder.

Divisible by 3

The digits add to a multiple of 3.

Adding the digits of 4,932 gives 4 + 9 + 3 + 2 = 18, and 3 divides 18. Yes: 4,932 ÷ 3 = 1,644

Why it works

This is the argument for 9 all over again. 9, 99 and 999 are multiples of 3 as well, being three 3s, thirty-three 3s and 333 of them, so the same split by place works: everything but the digits comes to 4,914 = 3 × 1,638, and the digits left behind add to 18 = 3 × 6. So the whole question rests on the 18, the division works out with nothing over, and 4,932 = 3 × 1,644.

The two tests read the same sum and can answer differently. 4,524 adds to 15, which 3 divides and 9 does not, so 4,524 ÷ 3 = 1,508 while 4,524 ÷ 9 = 502 r 6.

Six tests on one number

Any three- or four-digit number answers all six from its digits alone, and the divisions only confirm what the digits said.

A whole number with three or four digits.
  1. 2 The last digit is 2. Yes: 4,932 ÷ 2 = 2,466
  2. 5 The last digit is 2. No: 4,932 ÷ 5 = 986 r 2
  3. 10 The last digit is 2. No: 4,932 ÷ 10 = 493 r 2
  4. 4 The last two digits make 32. Yes: 4,932 ÷ 4 = 1,233
  5. 9 The digits add to 4 + 9 + 3 + 2 = 18. Yes: 4,932 ÷ 9 = 548
  6. 3 The digits add to 4 + 9 + 3 + 2 = 18. Yes: 4,932 ÷ 3 = 1,644

4,932 is a multiple of 2, 3, 4 and 9, and not of 5 or 10.

Beyond the syllabus

Divisible by 25

The last two digits are 00, 25, 50 or 75.

The last two digits of 4,932 make 32. No: 4,932 ÷ 25 = 197 r 7

Why it works

A hundred is four 25s, so the hundreds divide away cleanly and only the last two digits count. The 4,900 in 4,932 gives 49 × 4 = 196 twenty-fives, and the 32 holds one more with 7 left over: 4,932 ÷ 25 = 197 r 7.

Divisible by 8

The number made by the last three digits is a multiple of 8.

The last three digits of 4,932 make 932, and 932 ÷ 8 = 116 r 4. No: 4,932 ÷ 8 = 616 r 4

Why it works

A thousand is 125 eights, since 1,000 = 8 × 125, so the thousands never leave anything over and only the last three digits matter. In 4,932 those digits make 932, and 932 ÷ 8 = 116 r 4, so the whole division comes out as 4,932 ÷ 8 = 616 r 4.

A hundred is not a whole number of 8s, though: 100 = 8 × 12 + 4. That is why this test needs three digits where the test for 4 needs two. 132 ends in 32, which is 4 × 8, and passes the test for 4, but 132 ÷ 8 = 16 r 4.

Divisible by 6

The number passes the test for 2 and the test for 3.

4,932 ends in 2, and its digits add to 18. Yes: 4,932 ÷ 6 = 822

Why it works

6 = 2 × 3, and 2 and 3 have no common factor above 1, which is what makes this pair of tests safe to combine.

4,932 is even, so 4,932 = 2 × 2,466. Its digits add to 18, so 3 divides it as well. The 3 cannot be in the 2, so it must be inside the 2,466, and 2,466 = 3 × 822. Putting those together gives 4,932 = 2 × 3 × 822 = 6 × 822.

Divisible by 12

The number passes the test for 3 and the test for 4.

The digits of 4,932 add to 18, and its last two digits make 32. Yes: 4,932 ÷ 12 = 411

Why it works

12 = 3 × 4, and 3 and 4 have no common factor above 1, so passing both tests guarantees a multiple of 12. This is the same reasoning that took 2 and 3 to 6. 4,932 passes both, since its digits add to 18, which is 3 × 6, and its last two digits make 32, which is 4 × 8. So 4,932 ÷ 12 = 411.

2 and 6 will not do instead, because both of them contain a 2. 18 passes the tests for 2 and for 6, and 18 ÷ 12 = 1 r 6.

Divisible by 11

Add and subtract the digits alternately, starting from the right. If the answer is a multiple of 11, so is the number.

For 4,932: 2 − 3 + 9 − 4 = 4, and 11 does not divide 4. No: 4,932 ÷ 11 = 448 r 4

Why it works

Every place value lands within 1 of a multiple of 11, alternately under and over. Ten is 1 under eleven. A hundred is 1 over 99, which is 11 × 9. A thousand is 1 under 1,001, which is 11 × 91. That alternation is where the plus and minus in the test come from.

Splitting 4,932 that way gives 4 × 1,001 − 4 for the thousands, then 9 × 99 + 9 for the hundreds, then 3 × 11 − 3 for the tens, and finally the 2. The multiples of 11 add up to 4,004 + 891 + 33 = 4,928 = 11 × 448, and what is left over is 2 − 3 + 9 − 4 = 4. Since 4 is smaller than 11, it is the remainder itself, giving 4,932 ÷ 11 = 448 r 4.

Starting from the other end flips every sign, giving 4 − 9 + 3 − 2 = −4, and 11 divides −4 exactly when it divides 4. Either direction answers the test, but only the count from the ones digit hands back the remainder as well.

Divisible by 7

Remove the last digit, double it, and subtract the double from the number that is left. Repeat until the answer is small enough to recognise.

From 4,932, 493 − 4 = 489; from 489, 48 − 18 = 30, and 7 does not divide 30. No: 4,932 ÷ 7 = 704 r 4

Why it works

7 divides no power of ten: 10 ÷ 7 = 1 r 3, 100 ÷ 7 = 14 r 2 and 1,000 ÷ 7 = 142 r 6. So no tail of digits can be read on its own, and the test works on the whole number instead, shrinking it a step at a time.

Run it on 4,932 and the first step gives 493 − 2 × 2 = 489. Multiply that back up by 10 and it is 4,890, which sits 42 below 4,932, and 42 = 7 × 6. Two numbers a multiple of 7 apart leave the same remainder when divided by 7, so 7 divides 4,890 exactly when it divides 4,932. And since 4,890 is 10 × 489 and the 10 contributes no 7, everything now rests on the 489.

The doubling is chosen to land that gap on a multiple of 7. Whatever the last digit b, with a in front of it, 10 × (a − 2b) sits 21b below 10a + b, and 21 = 7 × 3. Run the step again on 489 and it gives 48 − 2 × 9 = 30, which 7 does not divide, so 4,932 ÷ 7 = 704 r 4.

Common mistakes

  • Calling 6 a multiple of 24 6 is a factor of 24, not a multiple of it. The multiples of 6 are the numbers counting in 6s reaches, 6, 12, 18, 24 and onwards, and none of them is smaller than 6.
  • Testing 2 on the digit sum The digits of 4,527 add to 18, which is even, and 4,527 is odd: 4,527 ÷ 2 = 2,263 r 1. Adding the digits tests 3 and 9; the test for 2 reads the last digit alone.
  • Testing 4 on the last digit alone 518 ends in 8, and 8 is a multiple of 4, but 518 ÷ 4 = 129 r 2. A ten is not a whole number of 4s, so the tens digit still counts: 18 is not a multiple of 4, while 516 passes because 16 is.
  • Testing 12 with the tests for 2 and 6 18 passes both, and 18 ÷ 12 = 1 r 6. Two tests can only be put together when their numbers have no common factor above 1: 3 and 4 give 12, and 2 and 3 give 6, but 2 and 6 both contain a 2.

Remember

  1. The multiples of 6 are 6, 12, 18, 24 and onwards, and 6 is a factor of every one of them.
  2. A common multiple of 4 and 6 is reached by both counts. 12, 24 and 36 are the first three, and 12, the first meeting, is the lowest common multiple.
  3. A test can read the last digits alone when some power of ten is a multiple of the divisor: one digit for 2, 5 and 10, two for 4 and 25, three for 8.
  4. 3 and 9 divide 9, 99 and 999, so their tests add the digits; 11 divides 11, 99 and 1,001, so its test adds and subtracts them alternately.
  5. 6 and 12 are two tests each, 2 with 3 and 3 with 4, and the pairs are safe because neither has a common factor above 1; 7 shrinks the number instead.
