  7. Factors and factor pairs

Before this page: be able to divide and see whether anything is left over from short division, and recognise the square numbers from recognising powers of a number.

Every factor has a partner

A factor divides a number exactly, leaving nothing over. Divide 24 by 3 and it hands back 8, so one trial has found two factors at once.

Pairs on the number line

A pair is two places on the line whose product is 24. Working up from 1 joins them one arc at a time, and each new arc sits inside the last, because both ends have moved inward. A trial that leaves a remainder joins nothing, so no arc is drawn from it.

List every factor of 24

Pairs that multiply to 24

Each trial that divides 24 exactly joins two places on the line.

24 has eight factors: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12 and 24

Where to stop

The arcs run out once the two ends meet. Anything above that point is the larger half of a pair whose smaller half has already been tried, so it can add nothing new.

Knowing where to stop

Tip

Find the next square number and take its root. Nothing above that root needs trying. For 24 the next square is 25, whose root is 5, so the trials are 1, 2, 3 and 4.

Example: list every factor of 45

Work up from 1, writing both ends of each pair.

  1. 7 × 7 = 49 The next square above 45 is 49, so the trials stop below its root of 7.
  2. 1 × 45 = 45 The first trial always divides, and it hands back the number itself.
  3. 3 × 15 = 45 2 leaves a remainder, so it joins no pair.
  4. 5 × 9 = 45 4 and 6 leave remainders too, and the six trials are done.
  5. 1, 3, 5, 9, 15, 45 Six factors, read off the two ends of three pairs.

When a factor is its own partner

36 is a square number. One trial there hands back the number it started from, so the arc for that pair has nowhere to travel.

List every factor of 36

Pairs that multiply to 36

The arcs nest as before, until one of them closes on a single point.

36 has nine factors: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18 and 36

36 has nine factors, not ten. 6 is written once rather than twice, and every square number counts an odd number of factors for the same reason.

Common mistakes

  • Losing the partner of a number already tried Trying numbers in no particular order drops one end of a pair. 3 was tried, so 8 belonged in the list beside it: 24 has eight factors, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24.
  • Leaving out the widest pair 1 divides every number, and every number divides itself, so 1 and 24 are on the list before any trial is made.
  • Counting a square number’s own partner twice 36 has nine factors, not ten. One place on the line stands for both ends of 6 × 6, so the 6 is written once: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36.

Remember

  1. A factor divides a number exactly, and the division hands back its partner.
  2. Working up from 1 fills the list from both ends, so nothing is missed or written twice.
  3. To know where to stop, find the next square number: nothing above its root needs trying.
  4. 24 has eight factors: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24.
  5. A square number has one factor that is its own partner, so it counts an odd number of factors.
