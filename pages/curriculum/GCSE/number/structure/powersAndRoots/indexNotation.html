What index notation means

Before this page: be able to multiply whole numbers, using long multiplication where they are large.

Index notation is a short way to write repeated multiplication of the same number. For example, 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 2^5.

The base is the repeated number. The index is the number of equal factors.

2^5

Base — 2
The repeated number. Written full size, on the line.
Index — 5
The number of equal factors. Written small and raised.

2^5 is read “two to the power of five”. The expression 2^5 is called a power. Some exam questions call the index the exponent; this page uses index throughout.

Note — an index of 1 means one factor, so 3^1 = 3. A number written with no index has an index of 1.

Working out a power

To evaluate a power, expand it into its equal factors and keep a running product. Multiply one factor at a time to keep the calculation manageable.

Example: work out 2^5

2⁵, read as 2 to the power of 5

The power to work out

Base 2, index 5.

2^5 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32

A power does not always have to be evaluated. Where a question asks for an answer in index form, 2^5 is the answer to give; where it asks for a value, work it out to 32.

Squared, cubed, and “to the power of”

An index of 2 is read as squared. A 5-by-5 square contains 5 × 5 = 25 unit squares: the side used twice, which is why the index is 2 and why 5^2 is called “5 squared”.

5^2 as an area

A 5 by 5 square

The square

Every edge is the same length, which is what makes it a square rather than any other rectangle.

5^2 = 5 × 5 = 25

Extending the idea from two dimensions to three dimensions, an index of 3 is read as cubed. A 4-by-4-by-4 cube contains 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 unit cubes: the side used three times, which is why the index is 3 and why 4^3 is called “4 cubed”.

4^3 as a volume

A 4 by 4 by 4 cube

The cube

Every edge is the same length, which is what makes it a cube rather than any other box.

4^3 = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64

Only powers 2 and 3 have these special names. Other indices are read “to the power of”: 2^5 is “two to the power of five”.

Squared and cubed are names for those two indices, not different operations. Every power is expanded and evaluated the same way.

Powers grow quickly

Each extra unit of the index multiplies by the base once more, so the value does not grow steadily — it multiplies. The powers of 2 double at every step.

The powers of 2, from index 1 to index 6
Power Expanded form Value
2^1 2 2
2^2 2 × 2 4
2^3 2 × 2 × 2 8
2^4 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 16
2^5 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 32
2^6 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 64

Changing the base changes every one of the factors, so it matters just as much: 3^4 is 81 while 2^4 is only 16.

Optional exploration — powers and dimensions

Squared is named after a square and cubed after a cube — but the table above ran on to an index of 6. What shape belongs to 4, or 5, or 6?

Each further index needs one more direction at right angles to the last: a useful picture, though not the definition of a power. Any number of such directions is mathematically well defined, although only three can exist in our universe.

Writing a product as a power

Every power on this page has been expanded into a product. Read the other way, a product of equal factors folds back into a power in two steps.

7 × 7 × 7 = 7^3

  1. The repeated factor is 7, so 7 is the base.
  2. There are three factors of 7, so 3 is the index.

Most products are not one number over and over. Where several numbers each repeat, each of them becomes a power of its own.

Example: write 2 × 2 × 5 × 5 × 5 in index form

2 × 2 × 5 × 5 × 5

Start from the product

Sorting the equal factors into groups is the first move.

2 × 2 × 5 × 5 × 5 = 2^2 × 5^3

Multiplication can be done in any order, so the factors may be rearranged to place equal factors together. Reorder them, count each group, then write the powers.

Example: write 3 × 5 × 3 × 5 × 3 in index form

Reorder, count, write.

  1. 3 × 5 × 3 × 5 × 3 The product as it is given, with the equal factors apart.
  2. = 3 × 3 × 3 × 5 × 5 The same five factors, reordered so that the equal ones stand together.
  3. = 3^3 × 5^2 Three 3s and two 5s, so the two counts are the two indices.

Common mistakes

  • Wrong idea — 3^4 = 12 Why it is wrong. The index counts four factors of 3; it is not multiplied by the base. Correct form. 3^4 = 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 81
  • Wrong idea — 2 × 2 × 2 = 2^2 Why it is wrong. There are three factors, even though there are two multiplication signs. Correct form. 2 × 2 × 2 = 2^3
  • Wrong idea — 5^2 = 10 Why it is wrong. Squared means multiply the number by itself; it does not mean double it. Correct form. 5^2 = 5 × 5 = 25
  • Wrong idea — 2^2 × 5^3 = 10^5 Why it is wrong. A power is one number multiplied by itself, so two different bases need two powers. Correct form. 2^2 × 5^3 = 4 × 125 = 500
  • Wrong idea — writing three squared as “32” Why it is wrong. On the baseline, this means thirty-two, not three squared. Correct form. 3^2 = 3 × 3 = 9

Remember

  1. Index notation is a short way to write repeated multiplication: the base is the number repeated, and the index is the number of equal factors.
  2. The index counts factors, not × signs, and a power is not the base times the index.
  3. Evaluate a power by expanding it and multiplying one factor at a time — or leave it in index form, where that is what the question asks for.
  4. Only index 2 and index 3 have special names, “squared” and “cubed”; every other index is read “to the power of”.
  5. Going the other way, gather equal factors into one power; different bases stay as separate powers.
