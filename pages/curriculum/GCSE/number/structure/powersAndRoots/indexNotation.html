Before this page: Be comfortable multiplying whole numbers.

A shorter way to write repeated multiplication

Suppose a number doubles, then doubles again, then doubles again. Starting with 1, that gives 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. A power keeps track of the repeated factor without writing it out every time.

2^3 = 2 × 2 × 2

Base: 2The number used as a factor

Index: 3How many copies of that factor

We read 2^3 as “2 to the power of 3”. The small raised number is the index, also called the exponent. It counts the factors, not the multiplication signs.

The position matters: 5^4 means four factors of 5, not the two-digit number 54.

Working out a power

For 2^5, multiply five factors of 2, one at a time: 2 × 2 = 4, then 4 × 2 = 8, then 8 × 2 = 16, and finally 16 × 2 = 32.

2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32

Five factors of 2 make 32.

Increasing the index by 1 adds another factor, not another 1 to the answer. For powers of 2, each step doubles the value. Changing the base changes every factor.

Multiplying the base by the index is a different calculation. For example, 3^4 = 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 81, whereas 3 × 4 = 12.

Squares and cubes

Two powers have familiar names. 4^2 is “4 squared”: a square with side length 4 cm has area 4 × 4 = 16 cm². 4^3 is “4 cubed”: a cube with edge length 4 cm has volume 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 cm³.

An index of 1 means just one factor: 7^1 = 7. For any positive whole-number index, powers of 1 stay at 1, and powers of 0 stay at 0.

Writing a product as powers

Going the other way, count the equal factors. The product 7 × 7 × 7 is 7^3. If there are different factors, collect each kind separately.

2 × 5 × 2 × 5 × 5

= (2 × 2) × (5 × 5 × 5)

= 2^2 × 5^3

Reordering the factors doesn't change the product. There are two factors of 2 and three of 5, so each base gets its own index.

Key points

  • The base is the repeated factor; the index counts its copies.
  • Evaluate a power by multiplying its factors, not by multiplying base and index.
  • Index 2 means squared; index 3 means cubed. Keep different bases separate when collecting factors.
