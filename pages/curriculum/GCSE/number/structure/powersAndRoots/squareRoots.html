The side of a square

Before this page: be able to square a number using index notation, and recall the squares to 15^2 from recognising powers of a number.

Squaring 7 gives 49. The square root of 49 is the number that was squared: 7.

49 unit squares pack into a square with 7 along every edge, so 7 is the side length that goes with an area of 49. A square root is that side.

49 as an area

A square of area 49

The area to work from

49 unit squares, packed into a square.

7 × 7 = 49, and √49 = 7

The sign for a square root is √, written with the number under its bar.

√49 = 7

  1. 49 stands under the sign, and it is the area of the square.
  2. 7 is the value of the root, and it is that square's side.

The symbol √x means the non-negative square root. Both 7 and −7 square to make 49, and the negative one is taken up on positive and negative square roots.

Reading the squares backwards

√196 asks which number multiplied by itself gives 196. The squares of 1 to 15 answer it: 196 stands against 14, so √196 = 14.

Example: work out √196

√196

The starting number

196, and the squares it is matched against.

√196 = 14, and 14 × 14 = 196

Example: work out √225

Match the number to a square, read the number that was squared, then square it back.

  1. √225 225 stands under the sign, so the question is which number multiplied by itself gives 225.
  2. 15 × 15 = 225 225 is the last of the squares of 1 to 15, and the number squared to make it is 15.
  3. √225 = 15 Squaring 15 returns the 225 that was under the sign, which is the check.

Halving 196 gives 98, and 98 × 98 = 9,604, which is 49 times 196. A root is the number that was squared, not half of the number under the sign.

Numbers between the squares

Every whole number either stands against one of the squares or falls between two of them.

A number standing against a square has a whole-number root; one between two squares has none. The two squares either side of a number fix where its root lies: 81 < 90 < 100 puts √90 between 9 and 10.

Roots of decimals

0.2 × 0.2 = 0.04, so √0.04 = 0.2. The figures come from the squares already known, since 4 is 2 squared, and only the point remains to be placed.

A product carries as many decimal places as its two factors together, a count taken from multiplying decimals, so squaring doubles that count and a root halves it.

√0.0016 = 0.04

  1. The figures are 16, and 16 is the square of 4.
  2. 0.0016 has four decimal places, so its root has two.

Example: work out √0.0016

√0.0016

The starting number

0.0016 under the sign, with four decimal places.

√0.0016 = 0.04, and 0.04 × 0.04 = 0.0016

Example: work out √1.44

Take the figures, find their root, then halve the count of decimal places.

  1. √1.44 1.44 has two decimal places, and its figures are 144.
  2. 12 × 12 = 144 144 is the square of 12, so the figures of the root are 12.
  3. √1.44 = 1.2 Half of two decimal places is one, so 12 is written as 1.2, and 1.2 × 1.2 = 1.44.

The halving needs an even count to work on: 0.5 has one decimal place, and half of one is not a whole number of places. Squaring the answer is what confirms the point has landed in the right column.

A root larger than its number

√0.0016 = 0.04 and √0.04 = 0.2 both came out larger than the number under the sign. Squaring a number between 0 and 1 makes it smaller: 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25, and reading that backwards puts the root above the number it came from.

0.25 as an area

A square of side 1

The square to work from

Side 1, area 1.

√0.25 = 0.5, the side of a square of area 0.25

Which side of 1 the number is on

√49 = 7 and √196 = 14 are both smaller than the number under the sign. Between 0 and 1 that reverses: √0.25 = 0.5, and 0.5 is larger than 0.25.

Common mistakes

  • Wrong idea: √196 = 98 Why it is wrong. 98 is half of 196, and halving is not rooting: 98 × 98 = 9,604. Correct form. 14 × 14 = 196, so √196 = 14
  • Wrong idea: √0.25 = 0.05 Why it is wrong. 0.05 × 0.05 = 0.0025, which keeps four decimal places instead of two. Correct form. 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25, so √0.25 = 0.5
  • Wrong idea: a square root is always smaller than its number Why it is wrong. Only above 1 is a root the smaller of the two. Between 0 and 1 the square is the smaller: 0.6 becomes 0.36. Correct form. √0.36 = 0.6, and 0.6 is larger than 0.36
  • Wrong idea: √(9 + 16) = √9 + √16 Why it is wrong. The bar covers the whole sum, so the left side is √25 = 5. Rooting the two terms separately gives 3 + 4 = 7. Correct form. √(9 + 16) = 5, while √9 + √16 = 7

Remember

  1. A square root is the side of a square with that area: √49 = 7, because 7 × 7 = 49.
  2. Squaring the answer returns the number that stood under the sign, which is the check on every root.
  3. The root of a square number is read backwards out of the squares of 1 to 15. A number between two consecutive squares, such as 50 between 49 and 64, has no whole-number root.
  4. Squaring doubles a decimal count, so a root carries half as many decimal places as the number under the sign: √0.0016 = 0.04.
  5. Between 0 and 1 a root is larger than the number it came from: √0.25 = 0.5.
