Reading a number backwards

Before this page: be able to read and work out a power, from index notation and powers.

Working out 2^5 is one direction. This page goes the other way: meeting 64 and recognising it as a power.

Two things make that possible. A handful of powers are worth knowing on sight, and any number can be tested against a base by dividing.

The square numbers

A square number is what a whole number gives when it is multiplied by itself. They are called square numbers because that many counters fill a square.

Each square is the one before it with a new row and a new column laid along two of its sides, and that addition is always the next odd number.

The cube numbers

A cube number is a whole number multiplied by itself and then by itself again, and that many counters fill a cube. The ones worth knowing are short.

The cubes of 1 to 5, and of 10
Power Value
1^3 1
2^3 8
3^3 27
4^3 64
5^3 125
10^3 1,000

Note — 10^3 is a thousand, which is worth holding on its own: it is the step between millimetres and metres, and between grams and kilograms.

The powers of 2, 3, 4 and 5

Beyond squares and cubes, four bases turn up often enough to be recognised. Read the table by column to learn one base, or by row to compare them at the same index.

The first five powers of 2, 3, 4 and 5

1 2 3 4 5
2 4 9 16 25
3 8 27 64 125
4 16 81 256 625
5 32 243 1,024 3,125

The powers of 2 are worth carrying further than the rest, because they run through place value, computing and doubling problems: 2^6 = 64, 2^7 = 128, 2^8 = 256, 2^9 = 512, 2^10 = 1,024.

One number, several powers

A number can be a power of more than one base. Sixty-four is the clearest case: the same 64 counters make a square of 8, a cube of 4, and a block halved six times.

64 counters, arranged three ways

64 counters

Sixty-four counters

Set out in no particular arrangement, they are just 64 things.

64 = 8^2 = 4^3 = 2^6

Nothing was added or taken away between those arrangements. Which power 64 is depends only on the base it is being read against, and a question that asks for a power of 2 wants 2^6.

Testing a number against a base

For a number not on the tables, divide by the base and keep dividing. Reaching 1 means the number is a power of that base, and the count of divisions is the index.

Stopping on a division that will not come out whole means it is not a power of that base at all.

Common mistakes

  • Wrong idea — 6^2 = 12 Why it is wrong. Squaring multiplies a number by itself, and the doubling is a different operation. Correct form. 6^2 = 6 × 6 = 36
  • Wrong idea — 64 is a power of 2, so it is not a power of 4 Why it is wrong. A number can be a power of several bases at once. Correct form. 64 = 8^2 = 4^3 = 2^6
  • Wrong idea — 100 is even, so it is a power of 2 Why it is wrong. Halving 100 reaches 25, which will not halve again, so the ladder stops above 1. Correct form. 100 is 10^2, and no power of 2 at all.
  • Wrong idea — 2^6 = 6^2 Why it is wrong. The base and the index do different jobs, so swapping them gives a different number. Correct form. 2^6 = 64 while 6^2 = 36

Remember

  1. The square numbers to 225 and the cubes of 1 to 5 and 10 are worth knowing on sight.
  2. Each square is the one before it plus the next odd number, so a forgotten entry can be rebuilt.
  3. The first five powers of 2, 3, 4 and 5 cover most of what a question will use.
  4. A number can be a power of more than one base: 64 is a square, a cube and a power of 2.
  5. To test any number, divide by the base until it reaches 1 or stops coming out whole.
