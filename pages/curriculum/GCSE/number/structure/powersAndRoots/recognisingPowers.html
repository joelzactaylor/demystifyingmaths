Most numbers are not powers

Before this page: be able to read and work out a power, from index notation and powers.

Of the first 256 whole numbers, sixteen are a power of 2, 3, 4 or 5. The rest are a power of nothing at all.

That is what makes them worth knowing on sight. A question that hands you 144 is usually handing you 12^2, and the work only starts once you have seen it.

The squares to 225

A square number is a whole number multiplied by itself, and that many counters fill a square.

Each one is the square before it with a row and a column added, and that addition is always the next odd number — which is how a forgotten entry is rebuilt from its neighbour.

A square of side 1 holds a single cell, so 1² = 1.

Note — every one of those is the number multiplied by itself, not doubled. 6^2 = 36, while doubling 6 gives 12.

The cubes worth knowing

A cube number is a whole number multiplied by itself twice over, and that many unit cubes fill a cube. There are far fewer to learn, and they climb much faster.

The cube is turned by dragging it, or with the arrow keys.

A cube of side 1, then 2, 3, 4 and 5

Cubes up to 5³

One cube

A cube of side 1 is a single unit cube, so 1³ = 1.

1, 8, 27, 64, 125

  • 1^3 = 1
  • 2^3 = 8
  • 3^3 = 27
  • 4^3 = 64
  • 5^3 = 125
  • 10^3 = 1,000

Note — 10^3 is a thousand: the step from millimetres to metres, and from grams to kilograms.

When one number is several powers

Sixty-four is a square, a cube and a power of 2 at the same time. Nothing is added or taken away between the three readings — the same 64 counters make all of them.

64 counters, arranged three ways

64 counters

Sixty-four counters

Scattered, they are just 64 things.

64 = 8^2 = 4^3 = 2^6

Swapping the two does not work, though: 2^6 is 64 while 6^2 is 36.

This happens whenever one base is itself a power of another. 4 is 2^2 and 8 is 2^3, so every power of 4 and every power of 8 is already a power of 2.

The powers of 2 are worth carrying furthest, because doubling turns up so often: 2^6 = 64, 2^7 = 128, 2^8 = 256, 2^9 = 512, 2^10 = 1,024.

Testing a number you do not recognise

With no base to test against, three questions settle it: is it one of the squares, is it one of the cubes, and does halving it reach 1?

Three questions settle it: is it a square, is it a cube, and does halving take it to 1?

What the lists cannot answer, dividing can: a number they both miss may still be a power further up, and the answer names every base that reaches it.

Common mistakes

  • Wrong idea — 6^2 = 12 Why it is wrong. Squaring multiplies a number by itself, and the doubling is a different operation. Correct form. 6^2 = 6 × 6 = 36
  • Wrong idea — 64 is a power of 2, so it is not a power of 4 Why it is wrong. A number can be a power of several bases at once. Correct form. 64 = 8^2 = 4^3 = 2^6
  • Wrong idea — 100 is even, so it is a power of 2 Why it is wrong. Halving 100 reaches 25, which will not halve again, so halving stops above 1. Correct form. 100 is 10^2, and no power of 2 at all.
  • Wrong idea — 2^6 = 6^2 Why it is wrong. The base and the index do different jobs, so swapping them gives a different number. Correct form. 2^6 = 64 while 6^2 = 36

Remember

  1. Most numbers are not a power of anything, which is what makes the short list of ones that are worth knowing on sight.
  2. The squares run to 15^2 = 225. Each is the one before plus the next odd number, so a forgotten entry can be rebuilt from its neighbour.
  3. The cubes worth knowing are 1, 8, 27, 64 and 125, and 10^3 = 1,000.
  4. One number can be a power of several bases: 64 is 8^2, 4^3 and 2^6, because 4 and 8 are themselves powers of 2.
  5. For anything else, check it against the squares, check it against the cubes, and halve it; dividing finds any base that reaches 1.
