Before this page: be able to read a base and its index from index notation and powers, and work out a root of a perfect square from square roots.

Does 3 + 4 × 5 come to 23 or to 35?

Two people can work the same five symbols and hand in different numbers. Gathering the 3 + 4 first leaves 7 × 5, which is 35; gathering the 4 × 5 first leaves 3 + 20, which is 23.

Neither of them has made a mistake: 3 + 4 is 7, and 4 × 5 is 20. All that separates the two answers is which pair went first, and so far nothing has settled that.

What happens with no agreed order

Three operations leave far more room to disagree. 2 + 3 × 4 − 1 can be worked from either end, or from the middle outwards, and the answers do not match.

5 × 4 − 1

20 − 1

19

right to left

2 + 3 × 3

2 + 9

11

the ends first

5 × 4 − 1

2 + 12 − 1

Four numbers, one calculation, and not a mistake among them: every step of every route is a correct calculation on the two numbers in front of it. A cake at £5 and three coffees at £4 is written 5 + 3 × 4. A till that gathered the 5 + 3 first would charge £32 for a £17 order.

So it is agreed, once and for everybody: multiplying and dividing are done before adding and subtracting. That picks 13 out of the four possible answers, and it settles 3 + 4 × 5 = 23 as well.

Example: work out 10 − 2 × 3

Take 2 × 3 from 10.

  1. The terms are 10 and 2 × 3. The minus sign separates them.
  2. 2 × 3 = 6 Two threes make one amount of 6.
  3. 10 − 2 × 3 = 4 Six taken from ten leaves four.

What a pair of brackets can move

Brackets are how to ask for the other reading. (3 + 4) × 5 = 35, because they make 3 + 4 the first step.

3 + 4 × 5 and (3 + 4) × 5

A rectangle 4 by 5

4 × 5 is a rectangle: four squares up the side, five along the bottom, 20 in all.

3 + 4 × 5 = 23, and (3 + 4) × 5 = 35

Moving one pair of brackets moves the answer a long way. 2 × 3 + 4 × 5 is 6 + 20 = 26, and bracketing 3 + 4 gives 2 × (3 + 4) × 5 = 70 instead.

2 × 3 + 4 × 5 = 26

Not every placing changes the answer. Brackets round a product change nothing, because it is gathered already: 2 × 3 + (4 × 5) = 26 as before.

Example: put brackets in 3 + 5 × 2 to make 16

Find the placing that gives 16.

  1. 3 + 5 × 2 = 13 Five twos make one amount of 10, and 3 + 10 = 13.
  2. Brackets round 3 + 5 make it the amount to be doubled. The 2 then multiplies the whole of 3 + 5 rather than the 5.
  3. (3 + 5) × 2 = 16 Eight taken twice.

Powers and roots before anything else

A power is repeated multiplication, so it goes before the multiplying around it. 3 × 4^2 is three sixteens, 48. A square root goes at the same point: 5 × √9 = 15.

2 + 3 × 4^2 ÷ 8

The power first

4 × 4 = 16, and by convention nothing else can be worked until it is.

2 + 3 × 4^2 ÷ 8 = 8

Neither × nor ÷ outranks the other, and the same holds for + and −, so each pair is worked from the left. 3 × 16 ÷ 8 = 48 ÷ 8 = 6, and 20 − 8 + 5 = 12 + 5 = 17.

2 + 3 × 4^2 ÷ 8 = 8

  • Brackets first, and the innermost pair before the pair around it.
  • Then powers and roots.
  • Then multiplying and dividing, from the left.
  • Then adding and subtracting, from the left.

BIDMAS is those four lines as six letters: brackets, indices, division, multiplication, addition, subtraction. The D and M are one strength and so are the A and S, so neither pair is necessarily worked in the order its letters happen to fall.

The same order under other names

Extra info

BODMAS is the older British form, its O standing for “of” or “order”; it is still usual in the United Kingdom and India. PEMDAS is taught in the United States, where brackets are parentheses and indices exponents. BEDMAS, with E for exponents, is usual in Canada and New Zealand.

The letters differ in order only to make a pronounceable word: BIDMAS puts D before M, PEMDAS puts M before D. Neither ordering is a ruling.

An implied multiplication is not written after an obelus

Extra info

A number written against a bracket, as in 8(1 + 3), is a multiplication with its sign left out. Standing after an obelus — the ÷ sign — it is read two ways, and no convention settles which. Taking ÷ and × as one strength from the left (strict BIDMAS) gives 4 ÷ 8 × 4 = 2; doing the implied multiplication first (IMF) gives 4 ÷ 32 = 0.125. Calculators and school courses mostly read it the first way, some physics and algebra texts the second.

So an implied multiplication should never be written after a ÷. Putting the division under a bar, or a bracket round the product, leaves one reading only: 4/(8(1 + 3)) = 0.125, or (4 ÷ 8)(1 + 3) = 2.

Style guides and ISO 80000-2, the international standard for mathematical notation, ask for that bracket or bar rather than settling which reading is right. Dave Peterson works through both conventions and lands in the same place in Implied Multiplication 1: Not as Bad as You Think (archived).

Example: work out 40 ÷ 5 × 2

Divide 40 by 5, then multiply by 2.

  1. Dividing and multiplying have the same strength. Neither outranks the other.
  2. 40 ÷ 5 = 8 The leftmost of the two comes first.
  3. 40 ÷ 5 × 2 = 16 Eight doubled. Multiplying first would give 40 ÷ 10 = 4, a different calculation.

Bars that group without brackets

A root's bar fixes how far the root reaches. √(9 + 16) has both numbers under one bar, so the 9 + 16 is added before the root is taken, and the answer is 5. Shortening the bar to cover the 9 alone leaves the 16 outside it, and √9 + 16 = 19.

A fraction bar groups the same way, one amount above it and one below, so (12 + 8)/(5 − 1) is 20 ÷ 4, which is 5. Either bar is a bracket that nobody has to write.

Example: work out (7 + 5)/(9 − 3)

Divide the amount above the bar by the amount below it.

  1. 7 + 5 = 12 The bar holds the whole of 7 + 5 together.
  2. 9 − 3 = 6 And the whole of 9 − 3 below it.
  3. 12 ÷ 6 = 2 The bar divides one amount by the other.

Common mistakes

  • Wrong idea: 2 + 6 × 3 = 24 Why it is wrong. This works from the left and adds 2 and 6 first. By convention the 6 × 3 is worked before the addition, so the amount added to 2 is 18. Correct form. 2 + 6 × 3 = 2 + 18 = 20
  • Wrong idea: 3 × 4^2 = 144 Why it is wrong. This multiplies first and squares 12. The index sits on the 4 alone, so the 4 is squared and the 3 multiplies the 16. Correct form. 3 × 4^2 = 3 × 16 = 48
  • Wrong idea: √(9 + 16) = 7 Why it is wrong. This takes the root of the 9 and of the 16 separately and adds 3 and 4. A root does not come apart over a sum: one bar runs over the whole of it, so the number under it is 25. Correct form. √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5
  • Wrong idea: 20 − 8 + 5 = 7 Why it is wrong. This adds 8 and 5 first and takes 13 from 20. Adding and subtracting have the same strength, so the 20 − 8 on the left is worked first. Correct form. 20 − 8 + 5 = 12 + 5 = 17

Remember

  1. By convention multiplying and dividing come before adding and subtracting: 3 + 4 × 5 = 3 + 20 = 23.
  2. Brackets are how to ask for a different grouping, so (3 + 4) × 5 = 35.
  3. Powers and roots are worked before the multiplying around them: 3 × 4^2 = 48.
  4. Operations of the same strength are worked from the left: 40 ÷ 5 × 2 = 16.
  5. A root's bar and a fraction bar group what is under them, so √(9 + 16) = 5.
