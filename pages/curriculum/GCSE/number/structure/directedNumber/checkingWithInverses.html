  7. Checking an answer with the inverse operation

Before this page: be able to add, subtract, multiply and divide with the written methods, and know a square root as the operation that undoes squaring from square roots.

Does 802 − 47 come to 845?

Working it out again runs the same steps in the same order, so a slip made the first time is made again and the two attempts agree. Undoing it tests the answer instead, and a wrong answer carried backwards has no reason to land where it started.

Example: 802 − 47 ≟ 845

Add back what was taken away.

  1. 845 + 47 The check starts from the answer, not from the numbers that made it.
  2. 845 + 47 = 892 Nothing here repeats the original working.
  3. 892 ≠ 802 The subtraction started at 802, so 845 cannot be its answer.

The check does more than disagree: the gap is the size of the slip. 892 overshoots 802 by 90, and 845 stands the same 90 above the answer the subtraction really has. Ninety is nine tens and no units, so the units digit is sound and the slip is in the tens column. The subtraction comes to 755, and 755 + 47 = 802 confirms it.

Which statement undoes the calculation

The shape of the calculation decides which statement tests it.

  • 38 + 56 ≟ 94 → 94 − 56 = 38
  • 94 − 56 ≟ 38 → 38 + 56 = 94
  • 24 × 15 ≟ 360 → 360 ÷ 15 = 24
  • 360 ÷ 15 ≟ 24 → 24 × 15 = 360
  • 6^2 ≟ 36 → √36 = 6

Every one of those pairings is the same move. The two end numbers change places, and the operation between them turns into its inverse: a subtraction into an addition, a division into a multiplication, a power into a root.

The ends change places

Swapping them and inverting the operation between them writes the check.

802 − 47 = 755 is checked by 755 + 47 = 802, 391 ÷ 23 = 17 by 17 × 23 = 391, and 4^3 = 64 by ³√64 = 4.

Writing the check

  1. Start from the answer, not from the numbers that made it.
  2. Apply the opposite operation, using one of the numbers that made it.
  3. The other one comes back if the answer is right.

That statement always tests the answer, and it is not the only one that does: 94 − 56 = 38 is confirmed by 38 + 56 = 94 and equally by 94 − 38 = 56. What never works is rearranging the three numbers into an unrelated calculation — one that is not tied to the original question.

Example: 17 × 23 ≟ 391

Undo the multiplication with a division.

  1. 391 ÷ 23 Start from the answer and divide by one of the numbers.
  2. 391 ÷ 23 = 17 The other number comes back, so the multiplication holds.

When one inverse statement is not enough

A division that leaves a remainder has two pieces of answer, and both have to go back in. The whole groups are multiplied and the remainder is added on afterwards.

Example: 47 ÷ 6 ≟ 7 r 5

Put the whole groups and the remainder back together.

  1. 7 × 6 = 42 The whole groups, multiplied back.
  2. 42 + 5 = 47 The remainder is added on, and 47 comes back.

What a root check leaves unsettled

A root undoes a power, but a square root hands back only the size of the number that was squared. Two numbers square to 49, and the root sign names one of them: √49 = 7, while (−7)^2 = 49 as well. So the check settles the size and says nothing about which of the two signs was meant.

Example: (−7)^2 ≟ 49

Test the size with a root, then settle the sign separately.

  1. √49 = 7 Undoing the squaring gives the size of the number that was squared.
  2. 7 ≠ −7 Not a disagreement. −7 is the same size as 7, and the root sign can only hand back one of the two.
  3. The index 2 is even. What the root cannot settle, the index does: an even index makes the answer positive whichever sign went in, so 49 is right.

A cube root keeps the sign of the number under it, so a cube needs no second step: ³√−64 = −4 checks (−4)^3 = −64 by itself. Only an even index hands back two numbers of the same size, and choosing between them is settled on positive and negative square roots.

Common mistakes

  • Wrong idea: 47 ÷ 6 = 7 r 5, checked by 7 × 6 = 42 Why it is wrong. This multiplies the whole groups back and stops, so the check lands on 42 and not on the 47 it started from. The remainder is part of the answer and has to go back in. Correct form. 7 × 6 + 5 = 47
  • Wrong idea: 94 − 56 = 38, checked by 56 − 38 = 18 Why it is wrong. This rearranges the three numbers into a calculation the original never made, so whatever it comes to says nothing about the answer. A test has to use the 38 as the answer it is checking. Correct form. 38 + 56 = 94
  • Wrong idea: 600 − 247 ≟ 447, checked by working it out again Why it is wrong. Running the same steps in the same order reproduces the same exchange slip, so 447 comes back a second time and looks confirmed. Correct form. 447 + 247 = 694 ≠ 600

Remember

  1. Redoing a calculation repeats its slips; undoing it does not.
  2. A check starts from the answer and applies the opposite operation.
  3. The gap between the check and the original number is the size of the slip.
  4. A remainder is part of the answer, so 7 × 6 + 5 = 47 and not 7 × 6 = 42.
  5. A square root gives back the size of a number and not its sign, so an even index leaves one more thing to settle; an odd index leaves nothing.
