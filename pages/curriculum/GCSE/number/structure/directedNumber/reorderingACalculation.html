Before this page: know which operation a calculation does first from order of operations, and be able to add and subtract a negative number from adding and subtracting negative numbers.

Choosing an easier order for the same calculation

A calculation need not be worked in the order it is written. A different order can make the arithmetic much easier without changing the answer, and 4 × 17 × 25 is a calculation worth rearranging.

4 × 17 × 25 has three numbers and two multiplications. Worked from the left it gives 4 × 17 = 68, and then 68 × 25, which needs writing out. Taking the 4 and the 25 together instead gives 100, and 100 × 17 = 1,700, which is easy to calculate.

Work out 4 × 17 × 25

A pair chosen for the round number it makes

4 and 25 multiply to 100, so taking that pair first leaves a multiplication by 100.

4 × 17 × 25 = 1,700

Nothing was added, dropped or altered on the way. The 25 kept its multiplication sign when it changed places, and every reordering has to do the same.

Which numbers can change places

Two numbers multiplied give the same product either way round. 4 × 6 and 6 × 4 both count the squares of one rectangle, and turning that rectangle a quarter turn changes which number counts the rows.

Three numbers work the same way. 8 × 7 × 5 can start from 8 × 7 = 56 or from 7 × 5 = 35, and 56 × 5 and 8 × 35 both come to 280. Adding behaves like this too: any two of the numbers being added can be paired first, and the total is unchanged.

Example: work out 47 + 86 + 53

Pair the two numbers that make a round number.

  1. 47 + 53 = 100 47 and 53 are the pair that reaches a hundred, and the 86 waits where it is.
  2. 100 + 86 = 186 Adding 86 to a hundred needs no column addition. Starting with 47 + 86 = 133 does.

The names for the two moves

Context

An operation is commutative when its two numbers can change places. 4 × 6 = 6 × 4 records that. It is associative when the pairing can move instead: 8 × 7 × 5 comes to 280 whether the 8 × 7 or the 7 × 5 is taken first. Adding and multiplying are both commutative and associative. A GCSE question asks for the reordering itself, not for the name of either property.

Where the order is fixed

Subtracting and dividing give a different answer the other way round. 9 − 4 = 5 while 4 − 9 = −5, and 20 ÷ 4 = 5 while 4 ÷ 20 = 0.2. In 9 − 4 the 9 is the number started from and the 4 is the number taken away, so swapping them swaps those two roles.

Changing which pair is taken first fails for the same two operations. (20 ÷ 4) ÷ 2 = 2.5, but pairing the last two instead gives 20 ÷ (4 ÷ 2) = 10. The ÷ 2 was meant to halve the answer; halving the divisor doubles the answer instead.

Example: work out 72 ÷ 6 ÷ 3

Divide from the left, without pairing the 6 and the 3.

  1. 72 ÷ 6 = 12 Neither × nor ÷ outranks the other, so with no brackets a line of them is worked from the left.
  2. 12 ÷ 3 = 4 Pairing 6 ÷ 3 = 2 first would divide 72 by 2 and reach 36, which is nine times too large.

A number moves with the sign in front of it

38 − 17 + 62 has a minus in it. Its numbers can still be reordered, because the minus belongs to the 17. The calculation adds 38, takes 17 and adds 62, and those three instructions can be carried out in any order. Moving the 17 on its own is what fails; moving −17 as a single number does not.

Work out 38 − 17 + 62

The minus travels with the 17

Sending −17 to the end leaves 38 and 62 side by side, and they make 100.

38 − 17 + 62 = 83

Reordering a calculation

  1. A number moves with the sign written in front of it, and never without it.
  2. Within a run of adding and subtracting, or a run of multiplying and dividing, those numbers may be written in any order, each keeping the sign in front of it.
  3. The rearranged line is still worked from the left, because reordering adds no brackets.
  4. The first number in a run carries a + in a sum and a × in a product, even though neither is written.

Moving a term is not the same as swapping two numbers. A term takes its sign with it, so every number goes on doing what it did before. Swapping the two numbers of a subtraction changes what each one does, which is why 4 − 9 is not another way of writing 9 − 4.

Example: work out 63 − 28 + 37

Send −28 to the end, so the two numbers being added meet.

  1. 63 + 37 − 28 −28 moves with its minus, which leaves the two numbers being added next to each other.
  2. 63 + 37 = 100 The pair that reaches a hundred is taken first.
  3. 100 − 28 = 72 28 is taken from a hundred at the end, so no exchanging is needed.

Cancelling before multiplying

A division sign travels with its number in the same way. 36 × 15 ÷ 9 is a run of multiplying and dividing, so the ÷ 9 can be brought forward to meet the 36. That gives 36 ÷ 9 = 4, and 4 × 15 = 60. What moves is the ÷ 9 and not the 9 on its own: 36 × 9 ÷ 15 = 21.6 leaves both signs where they were and answers a different question. Dividing first also keeps the numbers small; multiplying first gives 36 × 15 = 540 and turns the question into a three-digit division.

Example: work out 25 × 32 ÷ 8

Cancel before multiplying.

  1. 32 ÷ 8 = 4 The ÷ 8 moves to the 32, because 8 divides 32 exactly.
  2. 25 × 4 = 100 Multiplying first reaches 100 as well, but only through 25 × 32 = 800 and then 800 ÷ 8.

Common mistakes

  • Wrong idea: 24 − 31 written as 31 − 24 = 7 Why it is wrong. Subtracting is not commutative, so taking the smaller number from the larger answers a different question. 24 − 31 starts at 24 and takes 31 away, which carries it past zero to 7 below. Correct form. 24 − 31 = −7
  • Wrong idea: 38 − 17 + 62 reordered as 38 + 62 + 17 = 117 Why it is wrong. The 17 has moved and its minus has been left behind, so 17 is added where it should be taken away. Adding 17 instead of taking it away misses by 34, which is twice 17. Correct form. 38 + 62 − 17 = 83
  • Wrong idea: 72 ÷ 6 ÷ 3 worked as 72 ÷ (6 ÷ 3) = 36 Why it is wrong. 6 ÷ 3 is the easier pair to see, but dividing is not associative, so pairing it first divides 72 by 2 instead of dividing the answer by 3. The result is nine times the right one. Correct form. 72 ÷ 6 ÷ 3 = 4

Remember

  1. Adding and multiplying give the same answer whichever order their numbers are taken in.
  2. A number carries the sign written in front of it wherever it moves, so −17 travels as −17.
  3. Swapping the two numbers of a subtraction or a division changes the answer: 24 − 31 = −7 and not 7.
  4. Changing which pair of a division is taken first changes the answer too, so 72 ÷ 6 ÷ 3 is 4 and not 36.
  5. Choose the order for the arithmetic it makes: pairing 4 and 25 turns 4 × 17 × 25 into a multiplication by 100.
