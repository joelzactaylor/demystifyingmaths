Before this page: the column layout from column addition, and what each digit is worth in integers and decimals.

Subtracting in columns

Column subtraction takes one place value at a time. In 876 − 342 the ones ask 6 − 2 = 4, the tens ask 7 − 4 = 3 and the hundreds ask 8 − 3 = 5.

Each answer is written under the column it came from, and reading them together gives 534.

The layout works because every column of 876 − 342 holds enough to pay what is asked of it. 872 − 346 is set out the same way, but the ones column asks 2 − 6, and 2 ones cannot give 6.

100s 10s 1s 8 7 2 − 3 4 6 ?

One of the 7 tens is ten ones. Exchanging it gives 6 tens and 12 ones, and the total is still 872.

Exchanging without changing the value

872 = 800 + 70 + 2

872 = 800 + 60 + 12

Swapping a £10 note for ten £1 coins changes what can be paid, not how much money there is. An exchange does that, and it is available in every column: one hundred for ten tens, one tenth for ten hundredths.

The ones column now holds 12, and 12 − 6 = 6. The other two columns need no exchange: 6 − 4 = 2 and 8 − 3 = 5.

Two marks record the swap. Cross out the 7 and write 6 above it, because the tens gave one away. Write a small 1 in front of the 2, because the ones received it.

Every column asks the same question

Is the top digit big enough to take the digit below it?

Yes — subtract

Write the answer under the line and move one column left.

No — exchange first

Lower the column on the left by one, add ten to this column, then subtract.

Setting out a subtraction

Addition can be written either way round; subtraction cannot, because 8 − 3 and 3 − 8 are different questions. For the non-negative subtractions here, the larger number is written on top. If the number underneath is the larger one, the answer is negative, which adding and subtracting negatives covers.

Writing 14.6 as 14.60 makes the hundredths column explicit. There are zero hundredths before we exchange, so the 5 hundredths in 8.35 must be subtracted from an exchanged amount, not copied into the answer.

14.60

Work in one unit

Put both amounts in one unit before setting out. 1.2 m − 35 cm is set out as 1.20 − 0.35, and the answer 0.85 is in metres, because the columns count metres.

Whole numbers and decimals

In 6,145 − 2,978 the hundreds column gives its last hundred away, and then needs one from the thousands.

6,145 − 2,978

Align equal place values

Ones under ones, tens under tens, hundreds under hundreds.

Check it by adding

The answer added to the number taken away must return the starting number, so an exchange that was taken but never paid for shows up: using the crossed-out 4 gives 3,177, and 3,177 + 2,978 = 6,155, ten too large.

Once the points are lined up, the columns carry on past them, and the answer's point goes beneath them.

Finding what is left or how far apart

Removing 8.35 m from 14.6 m asks what remains. Comparing 2.4 m with 1.75 m asks for the distance between them. Both structures lead to subtraction.

What is left

A roll holds 14.6 m of cable and 8.35 m is cut off it. What remains is 14.60 − 8.35 = 6.25 m.

Change from a payment is the same shape of question: the money handed over is the starting amount, and the price comes out of it.

How far apart

One shelf is 2.4 m long and another is 1.75 m. Nothing is cut; the question is the gap between them, which is 2.40 − 1.75 = 0.65 m.

0.65 m 1.75 m 2.4 m

For these problems, subtract the smaller amount from the larger to find the gap. Decide what the amounts represent before choosing the calculation.

Key points

  • Line up the decimal points and fill empty decimal places with zeros.
  • Work from right to left. Exchange one unit of the next place for ten of the current place when needed.
  • Record both sides of an exchange; the original number has not changed.
  • Check by adding the answer to the amount subtracted.
