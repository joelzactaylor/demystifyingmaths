Before this page: know the multiplication tables to 12 × 12 and understand the value of each digit from place value.

Sharing one place at a time

Short division breaks 984 ÷ 4 into smaller divisions, working from hundreds to tens to ones. Each answer digit goes above the place it counts.

Read the layout as 984 divided by 4

In 4984, 4 is the divisor, 984 is the dividend and the quotient is written above the line.

For a two-digit divisor, recording the subtractions can help. That gives the method called long division; a decimal divisor is first made a whole number, by the method on dividing by decimals.

Exchanging the remainder

In 984 ÷ 4, the 9 is 9 hundreds. Shared into 4, they give 2 hundreds each, with 1 hundred left that cannot be shared as a hundred:

  • 1 hundred → 10 tens

    10 tens and the next 8 tens make 18 tens: 18 ÷ 4 = 4 tens, with 2 tens left.

  • 2 tens → 20 ones

    20 ones and the next 4 ones make 24 ones: 24 ÷ 4 = 6.

The small carried numerals record those exchanges: the 1 written before the 8 is the 1 hundred remainder renamed as 10 tens, and 984 itself is unchanged.

984 ÷ 4

Set out 984 ÷ 4

984 is 9 hundreds, 8 tens and 4 ones.

The short-division method

Work from the greatest place to the smallest

  1. 1

    Divide the current amount by the divisor.

  2. 2

    Write the whole-number result directly above that place.

  3. 3

    Carry any remainder in front of the next digit.

  4. 4

    Move right and repeat until every written digit has been used.

Begin with the first digit, or the first two if the first is smaller than the divisor

In 984 ÷ 4, 9 ≥ 4, so begin with 9. In 132 ÷ 8, 1 < 8 but 13 ≥ 8, so begin with 13 and write nothing above the 1.

A remainder is always smaller than the divisor

If it is not, another whole group can still be made. For example, 18 ÷ 4 is 4 remainder 2, not 3 remainder 6.

Keeping zeros in the quotient

After the first answer digit has been written, each remaining dividend digit must produce a digit in the quotient, even when that digit is 0:

6,048 ÷ 6

Set out 6,048 ÷ 6

6,048 is 6 thousands, 0 hundreds, 4 tens and 8 ones.

Dividing a decimal

The decimal point is not divided or carried. Put the point in the quotient directly above the point in the dividend, then continue through the decimal places with the same routine. If the quotient is below 1, write a zero before its decimal point.

18.9 ÷ 7

Set out 18.9 ÷ 7

18.9 is 1 ten, 8 ones and 9 tenths.

Remainder or decimal

First use every digit already written in the dividend. If the final remainder is zero, the division is complete. If it is not, the question says which to give: the answer with a remainder, or an exact decimal. For a worded problem the context decides, as on interpreting remainders.

Route 1

Stop with a remainder

Record the whole-number quotient and the remainder from the last original digit.

985 ÷ 4 = 246 remainder 1

Route 2

Continue as a decimal

Exchange the remainder into the next decimal place and carry on dividing.

985 ÷ 4 = 246.25

Continuing into decimals

If the original digits run out and the question requires an exact decimal, extend the dividend with a zero after its decimal point. Add the point first if the dividend is a whole number. This does not change its value: 13 = 13.0 = 13.00 = 13.000.

Example: calculate 13 ÷ 8 exactly

13 ÷ 8

Set out 13 ÷ 8

13 is 1 ten and 3 ones.

13 ÷ 8 is terminating because the remainder reaches zero. If the remainder never reaches zero the decimal repeats instead: 13 ÷ 3 = 4.333…, written in recurring-decimal notation.

Checking by multiplication

Multiply the quotient by the divisor

  • For an exact answer: 246 × 4 = 984 and 1.625 × 8 = 13.
  • For an integer remainder, add it back: 246 × 4 + 1 = 985.

Multiplication checks both the digits and their places. For example, 5,035 ÷ 5 = 1,007, and 1,007 × 5 = 5,035. If we dropped both zeros and wrote 17, multiplying back would give only 85.

Key points

  • Divide from left to right, keeping each quotient digit in its place.
  • Exchange a remainder into the next smaller place before continuing.
  • Keep any internal zeros, and place the quotient’s decimal point above the dividend’s.
  • Zeros after the decimal point let an unfinished division continue.
