  7. Dividing by a decimal

Before this page: know how to multiply by powers of ten, then divide with short division or long division.

Make the divisor an integer without changing the quotient

Standard written division needs an integer divisor. A decimal divisor can be made into an integer by multiplying it by 10, 100 or 1,000—but the dividend must be multiplied by exactly the same number.

a ÷ b = (a × k) ÷ (b × k)

The quotient counts how many divisor-sized groups make the dividend. Multiplying both by the same scale factor, 100, makes every group 100 times larger and the total 100 times larger, so the number of groups does not change.

In 55.2 ÷ 0.46, 120 groups of 0.46 make 55.2. After both numbers are multiplied by 100, the same 120 groups of 46 make 5,520.

55.2 ÷ 0.46

The divisor 0.46 has two decimal places

One multiplication by 10 is not enough to make 0.46 an integer.

Choose the smallest sufficient power of ten

Count the divisor's decimal places, ignoring trailing zeros, and multiply both numbers by 10 once for each place: one place needs ×10, two need ×100. The divisor is then an integer, and the equivalent division is ready for short or long division.

both × 10

One decimal place

7.2 ÷ 0.3 = 72 ÷ 3 = 24

both × 100

Two decimal places

1.8 ÷ 0.04 = 180 ÷ 4 = 45

both × 10

The dividend may stay decimal

8.75 ÷ 2.5 = 87.5 ÷ 25 = 3.5

both × 10

Ignore a trailing zero

6.4 ÷ 0.20 = 64 ÷ 2 = 32

0.20 is 0.2, so one multiplication by 10 is enough.

The quotient can be larger than the dividend

55.2 m total
120 equal lengths, each 0.46 m

55.2 metres cut into 0.46 metre lengths

Each length is under half a metre, so more than two fit into each metre, and exactly 120 fit into 55.2 m.

For positive numbers, dividing by a positive number below 1 makes the quotient larger than the dividend: 55.2 ÷ 0.46 = 120. Multiplying by a positive number below 1 has the opposite effect, explained on numbers below one.

Multiply both by 100

12.6 ÷ 0.35→1,260 ÷ 35

12.6 ÷ 0.35 = 36

0.35 has two decimal places, so both numbers are multiplied by 100.

Common mistakes

  • Scaling only the divisor Multiplying 0.24 by 100 and leaving the 9.6 alone gives 9.6 ÷ 24 = 0.4, a hundred times too small, because only the divisor took the ×100; with both numbers scaled, 960 ÷ 24 = 40.
  • Stopping while the divisor is still decimal One multiplication by 10 takes 3.75 ÷ 0.15 to 37.5 ÷ 1.5, and dividing that as though it were 37.5 ÷ 15 gives 2.5, ten times too small. A second ×10 on both numbers gives 375 ÷ 15 = 25.
  • Expecting the answer to be smaller Eight 0.12s make only 0.96, so more than eight fit into each 1, and 4.8 ÷ 0.12 = 40 is larger than 4.8.

Summary

  1. Choose the smallest power of ten that makes the decimal divisor an integer.
  2. Multiply the dividend and divisor by exactly the same power of ten.
  3. Stop once the divisor is an integer; the dividend may still be decimal.
  4. Use short or long division on the equivalent calculation.
  5. Check by multiplying the quotient by the original divisor: 120 × 0.46 = 55.2.
  6. For positive numbers, a divisor below 1 gives a quotient larger than the dividend: 55.2 ÷ 0.46 = 120.
