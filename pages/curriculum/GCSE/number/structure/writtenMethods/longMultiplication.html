Before this page: know the multiplication tables to 12 × 12, be able to add in columns, and know what each digit is worth in integers and decimals.

One large product, many small products

347 × 26 is built from table facts such as 6 × 7, 6 × 4 and 2 × 3.

Multiplying by 26 is multiplying by 20 and by 6 and adding the results, and 347 splits the same way, into 300, 40 and 7.

Each of 300, 40 and 7 is multiplied by each of 20 and 6: six small multiplications.

Every part of 347 meets every part of 26

A rectangle gives each of the six products a box of its own. 300, 40 and 7 run along the top, 20 and 6 down the side, and each box holds the product of its row and its column.

The grid: 347 × 26

347 × 26

Split 347 into its parts

347 is 300 + 40 + 7. Each digit is written as the amount it stands for, so nothing about the number has changed.

The six boxes are independent and can be calculated in any order.

Each box becomes a line in the columns it is worth

347 × 26

Six boxes, six lines

Each of the six products becomes a line in columns, its digits under the places they are worth.

Six lines, one for each product, show the expanded calculation behind long multiplication. The written layout takes the 6 of the multiplier before the 20, and within each, 7 then 40 then 300. Within each group of three, every line ends in one more zero than the one before it: 42, 240, 1,800, then 140, 800, 6,000.

Every line can be checked against its box.

The lines from one digit add to one row

347 × 26

Six lines, three of them for each digit

The three lines from the 6 can be added on their own, and so can the three from the 20.

Gathering the lines leaves one row for each digit of 26, and the six products are added in two stages instead of one.

The row from the tens digit ends in a zero

The three lines that came from the 20 are 140, 800 and 6,000. Every one of them ends in a zero, because every one of them is 20 times something. Their total, 6,940, is a sum of multiples of 10, so it ends in a zero too.

347 × 2 = 694: the 4 in the ones is from 2 × 7 = 14.

347 × 20

3 4 7 × 2 0 6 9 4 0

Ten times as much, so the same three digits sit one place further left and a zero holds the ones.

A third digit in the multiplier is a hundreds digit, so all of its lines end in two zeros and so does its row. The pattern continues for as many digits as the multiplier has: one more zero for each place.

The short form writes each row directly and carries

The short form produces those two rows without ever writing the lines that make them. Instead of 42, 240 and 1,800 written as lines, 6 × 7, 6 × 4 and 6 × 3 are written straight into one row, digit by digit. Anything too large for one column is carried into the next.

The rows are 347 × 6 and 347 × 20, and 6 + 20 = 26, so together they are 347 × 26.

Set the two numbers out

The six products, the two rows and the answer are unchanged. The six lines are no longer written, so none of them can be checked against its box.

A three-digit multiplier makes three rows

248 × 135 has three digits in the multiplier, so three rows, and the third starts two places further left because the 1 is worth 100. Three parts against three parts is nine products, gathered into three rows.

Where it turns up

Worded questions do not always say which operation to use. Equal groups and rectangular area are two common structures that lead to multiplication.

Many of the same thing

A coach company charges £24 a seat and 37 seats are booked. The cost is 24 × 37 = £888.

Anything counted in equal amounts has this shape: boxes of the same size, wages for the same hourly rate, identical parts on an order.

A rectangle

A worktop is 246 cm long and 84 cm deep. Both measurements are in the same unit, so its area is in that unit squared: 246 cm × 84 cm = 20,664 cm².

The equal-group or rectangular structure—not one keyword—makes multiplication appropriate. The context also supplies the answer's unit, which the columns alone cannot give.

Common mistakes

  • The missing place-holder zero A second row without its zero is ten times too small, and a third row without its two zeros is a hundred times too small. In 347 × 26 the second row is 6,940, not 694, and without the zero the total reads 2,776 instead of 9,022.
  • Adding the carry before multiplying In 347 × 6 the ones give 42, so 4 is carried. The tens are then 6 × 4 = 24, and the carried 4 joins afterwards to make 28. Taken as (4 + 4) × 6 = 48, the row reads 2,282 instead of 2,082.
  • Carrying between rows Each row starts with nothing carried into it. In 347 × 26 the 4 carried in the ones row still sits above the tens digit. Adding it again in the tens row turns 4 × 2 + 1 = 9 into 13, the row into 7,340 and the total into 9,422.
  • Losing one of the products Three digits over two is six products, and three over three is nine. Drop 40 × 20 = 800 from the grid and the total reads 8,222, from five products instead of six.

Summary

  1. 347 × 26 is six small products: every part of 347 against every part of 26.
  2. A grid gives each product a box. A column layout gives each one a line, placed in the columns it is worth.
  3. The lines belonging to one digit of the multiplier add together to make one row.
  4. A row made by the tens digit ends in one zero, and one made by the hundreds digit ends in two, because every line in it did.
  5. The short form writes each row directly, carrying instead of setting the lines out, and then adds the rows in columns.
