  7. Multiplying decimals

Before this page: be able to multiply whole numbers with long multiplication, know what each digit is worth in integers and decimals, and know what happens to a number when it is multiplied or divided by ten.

The same digits at a different size

37 × 26 = 962, and 3.7 × 2.6 = 9.62 — the same three digits, with a point dropped into them.

The whole-number product supplies the digits. Place value supplies their size: 962, 96.2, 9.62 and 0.962 use the same digits at four different scales.

Multiplying by 10 moves the digits one place left past the point: 3.7 × 10 = 37 and 2.6 × 10 = 26.

3.7 × 2.6

3.7 × 2.6

The digits are those of 37 × 26, an ordinary multiplication; the points are the only difference.

The two ×10s on the factors and the ÷100 on the product cancel.

Every decimal multiplication goes the same way, both numbers made whole and the answer put back; only the power of ten changes.

Each decimal place in the question is one place in the answer

Each decimal place across the two factors contributes one factor of 10 when the numbers are made whole.

Count the places, multiply the digits, place the point

  1. 1

    Count the decimal places in the question.

  2. 2

    Ignore the points and multiply the digits.

  3. 3

    Place the point to give that many places.

An estimate checks the count: rounding 3.7 × 2.6 to 4 × 3 = 12 shows that 9.62 is the right size and 96.2 is not.

Points line up for adding, not for multiplying

In an addition, tenths are added to tenths, so the points have to line up in one column. In a multiplication every digit on top meets every digit underneath, so the points are not lined up: 37 and 26 are written as any two whole numbers are, ones under ones.

3.7 + 2.6, adding

3.7 + 2.6 6.3

3.7 × 2.6, multiplying

37 × 26 962

No points at all: 37 × 26 = 962, then two places counted back give 9.62.

No need to pad the shorter number

4.75 × 0.6 becomes 475 × 6. Padding 0.6 out to 0.60 so the two match is right for adding.

In a multiplication it only makes work: the calculation becomes 475 × 60, with four places to count back instead of three and one more zero to tidy off at the end.

The answer can be smaller than both numbers

0.3 × 0.4 = 0.12, and 0.12 is smaller than both factors. Multiplying by 0.4 takes four tenths of 0.3, so the product is part of the original amount.

0.3 across, 0.4 down

0.3 × 0.4

One whole square

The square is 1 along the top and 1 down the side, so its area is 1 × 1 = 1.

For a positive first number, a positive multiplier above 1 makes the product larger; a multiplier between 0 and 1 makes it smaller.

A trailing zero comes off only after the places are counted

0.20 and 0.2 are the same number, so 0.5 × 0.4 is written 0.2 — but only after both places have been counted back:

0.5 × 0.4

0.5 × 0.4

Both numbers are below 1, so the answer comes out smaller than either; the digits still multiply as whole numbers, 5 × 4.

Two zeros turn up in that calculation doing opposite jobs: the zero before the point holds the ones column, so standard notation writes 0.2 rather than .2, and the zero on the end holds nothing.

Where it turns up

Decimals in a question are usually money or measurements, and both come with a unit that the answer must carry.

Money

Six tins at £4.35 each. That is 435 × 6 = 2,610 with two places to come back, so £26.10.

The last zero stays: prices are written to the penny, so £26.10, not £26.1.

Area

A rug 2.4 m by 1.5 m. That is 24 × 15 = 360 with two places to come back, so 3.6 m².

Both measurements must be in the same unit: both are metres, so the area is in m².

The count of places is the same whatever is being measured; the unit decides only whether a trailing zero is kept: £26.10, but 3.6 m².

Multiply two chosen numbers

72 × 34 = 2,448 gives both 7.2 × 3.4 = 24.48 and 0.72 × 0.34 = 0.2448: one multiplication, then two places counted back and then four.

6.3 × 0.45

Common mistakes

  • Bringing the point straight down Set out on their points like an addition, 3.7 × 2.6 still multiplies as 37 × 26 = 962, and a point copied down under the others lands on 96.2: one decimal place where the question has two. The count gives 9.62.
  • Losing a zero that holds a place 0.2 × 0.3 is 6 with two places, which gives 0.06. Writing 0.6 is ten times too big: a zero has to go into the tenths column to push the 6 out into the hundredths.
  • Tidying before counting 0.5 × 0.4 gives 20, and both places are counted back before the zero on the end comes off. Taking the zero off first leaves 2, and two places on that is 0.02 instead of 0.2.
  • Expecting the answer to be larger 6.3 × 0.45 = 2.835, under half of 6.3. A point placed to make the answer larger than 6.3, at 28.35, leaves two decimal places where the question has three.

Summary

  1. A decimal multiplication is a whole-number multiplication with the point put back afterwards.
  2. Making both numbers whole multiplies the calculation by a power of ten, and dividing the answer by the same power puts it back.
  3. The answer has as many decimal places as the question: count them across both numbers, then place the point.
  4. For positive numbers, multiplying by a positive number below 1 gives less than the first number, because only part of that number is taken.
  5. Count the places first, then tidy any zero off the end — never the other way round.
