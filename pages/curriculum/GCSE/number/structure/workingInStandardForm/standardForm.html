Introduction

Before this page: be able to multiply and divide by powers of ten, and read a power carrying a negative index.

Standard form exists because the alternative is counting zeros, and counting zeros is where the mistakes come from. Writing a number as digits multiplied by a power of ten separates the two things a large or small number is carrying: how big it is, and what its digits are.

The rule

a × 10n

  • a is at least 1 and less than 10. One non-zero digit sits in front of the decimal point — never two, never none.
  • n is a whole number. It may be negative, and a negative n makes the number small, not negative.
  • Both conditions have to hold. Break either one and the value may still be right, but the form is wrong.

Worked examples

Three methods, set out as a mark scheme would set them out. The right-hand column is the part worth reading twice: it is the reason each step is allowed.

Writing an ordinary number in standard form

Write 0.000 62 in standard form.

  1. 0.000 62 Find the first non-zero digit. That digit has to end up in front of the point.
  2. 6.2 Move the point until exactly one non-zero digit sits in front of it.
  3. 4 places, to the right Count the move. Moving right means the original was smaller than the digits alone suggest.
  4. 6.2 × 10−4 A move to the right makes the power negative. Multiplying by 10−4 undoes the move, so the value is unchanged.

Multiplying

Work out (3 × 105) × (8 × 10−2).

  1. 3 × 8 = 24 Multiply the numbers in front of the powers.
  2. 105 × 10−2 = 103 Add the powers. This is the ordinary index law, not a separate rule for standard form.
  3. 24 × 103 The value is now right, but the form is not: 24 is not below 10.
  4. 2.4 × 104 Move the point one place left and raise the power by one. One cancels the other, so the value does not change.

Adding, where the powers differ

Work out 4.1 × 106 + 3.5 × 105.

  1. 106 against 105 The powers differ, so nothing can be added yet. Powers are added when multiplying, never when adding.
  2. 4.1 × 106 = 41 × 105 Rewrite one of them so both carry the same power. Matching the smaller power is usually less work.
  3. 41 + 3.5 = 44.5 With a common power the numbers in front can finally be added, exactly as with common units.
  4. 44.5 × 105 = 4.45 × 106 Put the result back into standard form. The last mark is nearly always for this step.

Where marks are lost

Four mistakes account for most of the marks dropped on standard form. Every one of them is tested after the lesson that teaches the corresponding method.

  • “23 × 104 is standard form” It is the right value in the wrong form. The number in front must be at least 1 and less than 10, so the answer is 2.3 × 105.
  • “A negative power means a negative number” It makes the number smaller than 1, not negative. 5 × 10−3 is 0.005, which is positive.
  • “Adding the numbers means adding the powers” 3 × 104 + 3 × 104 is 6 × 104, not 9 × 108. Powers are added when multiplying only.
  • “The biggest digits mean the biggest number” 9.9 × 105 is smaller than 1.1 × 106. Compare the powers first, and the digits only when the powers match.
