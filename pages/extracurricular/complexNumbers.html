Introduction

Numbers are one of the first tools we learn. We use them to count, measure and compare. For most of us, that means using numbers on a straight line: positive to the right, negative to the left. Those are called real numbers.

This page takes that familiar idea and makes it a little more generous. Instead of only moving left and right, we also allow numbers to move up and down. That extra direction is what makes the world of complex numbers interesting, useful and surprisingly easy to understand.

The Real Number Line

Imagine a long straight road with zero in the middle. Every number has one place on this road.

Drag the point, or click anywhere on the line to place it.

This number line is a very simple idea, but it is powerful. The further right you go, the larger the number. The further left you go, the smaller it gets. Every point on the line corresponds to exactly one real number.

Real numbers appear in everyday situations like measuring length, money, temperature and speed. They are the kind of numbers that feel natural because they can be represented on this straight line.

But what if there was more space than just left and right?

From the Line to the Plane

Now imagine the number line as the horizontal direction of a flat surface. The extra direction is vertical. That gives us a whole new playground called the complex plane.

When the dot moves off the line, the number can no longer be described by a single value. Instead, it becomes a pair of values:

z = a + b i

Here, a is the real part and controls the left-right position. The number b is the imaginary part and controls the up-down position.

For example, z = 3 + 4i sits 3 units to the right and 4 units up from the origin.

The letter i is the imaginary unit. It is a new direction, similar to how 1 is the one-step in the real direction. One step up is i, one step down is -i, and one step left is -1.

Although the name sounds strange, imaginary numbers are not make-believe. They are a useful way to describe real phenomena, such as:

  • alternating electrical current, where voltage and current swing back and forth;
  • sound and radio waves, where the timing or phase of a wave matters as much as its size;
  • rotations and turning motions in animation and engineering;
  • systems that respond over time, like the speed control in an electric motor.

Because complex numbers use two parts, they behave like coordinates on a plane. That is why mathematicians say complex numbers are like points in a 2D space.

Polar Form

An alternate way to describe a point in the plane is by how far it is from the origin and the direction it points in. This is called polar form.

In Cartesian form we use the horizontal and vertical distances. In polar form we use:

  • r: the distance from the origin, also called the magnitude or modulus;
  • θ: the angle from the positive real axis, also called the argument or phase.

For complex numbers, polar form is written as:

z = r cis θ

That means "r times cosine plus i sine" in a compact way. More formally, the connection between all three forms is:

z = a + b i = r ( cos θ + i sin θ ) = r e i θ

If you are not comfortable with radians yet, there is a helpful explanation on my page about angle units.

To convert from rectangular form to polar form:

r = a 2 + b 2

θ = atan2 ( b , a )

Then to convert from polar back to rectangular form:

a = r cos θ b = r sin θ

Addition and Subtraction of Complex Numbers

The easiest way to understand adding and subtracting complex numbers is by moving the points on the plane.

z = z₁ + z₂

When you add complex numbers, you add the horizontal parts together and the vertical parts together. The result is the opposite corner of a parallelogram formed by the two input points and the origin.

For subtraction, the same idea works, but one corner is reversed, so the resulting vector points from the second number toward the first.

In symbols, if

z 1 = a 1 + b 1 i

and

z 2 = a 2 + b 2 i

then

z = z 1 + z 2 = ( a 1 + a 2 ) + ( b 1 + b 2 ) i

And the difference is:

z = z 1 - z 2 = ( a 1 - a 2 ) + ( b 1 - b 2 ) i

Multiplication and Division of Complex Numbers

Multiplying complex numbers is not just about size; it is also about turning. One number affects the other by stretching or shrinking it and by rotating it.

z = z₁ × z₂

Try moving the points and notice how the result changes. In polar form, the rule becomes quite simple.

Try to figure out the rule!

When you multiply two complex numbers, their magnitudes (how far from the origin they are) are multiplied, and their arguments (the directions they point) are added.




This geometric idea explains a famous fact about complex numbers.

Try putting both input dots on the point i.

Then ask yourself: what does i × i equal?

If you prefer to see the same idea in algebra, you can use FOIL to multiply two complex numbers.

Using FOIL, the multiplication expands to:

( a 1 + b 1 i ) × ( a 2 + b 2 i ) = a 1 a 2 + a 1 b 2 i + b 1 i a 2 + b 1 i b 2 i

Since i² = −1, the last term becomes negative, and the result becomes:

( a 1 a 2 − b 1 b 2 ) + ( a 1 b 2 + a 2 b 1 ) i

Let's see this with a concrete example. If we multiply (1 + i) × (1 + i):

= 1 + i + i + i 2 = 1 + 2 i − 1 = 2 i

Geometrically, we started with a point at 1 + i.
(45° away from the origin, at distance √2).
Multiplying by itself means rotating by 45° again (to reach 90°) and stretching by a factor of √2 (so the distance becomes 2).
The result is 2i, which sits 2 units straight up—exactly what we expect!

Division of complex numbers is the reverse of multiplication. In the world of directions, dividing one number by another means dividing how far it is from the origin and subtracting the angles.

That's why the polar form is so useful: it makes division feel like undoing a rotation and a stretch.

Exponentiation of Complex Numbers

'Exponentiation' means raising one number to the power of another. For complex numbers, that can create surprising patterns.

The shaded regions show the positions where the point can move while keeping the output inside the outer circle. They are a visual guide to the "safe" inputs for this simulation. I added them because the pattern can otherwise be very hard to spot. (If you find a simple one that describes all the behaviour well, please let me know!)

z = z₁^z₂

The shape of the result depends on where the points are placed. In many regions, you will see the famous logarithmic spiral appear.

This is fascinating because the same spiral appears in nature and art. Complex exponentiation turns a simple algebraic idea into something beautifully curved.

Try moving the blue dot to the origin, then moving the orange dot to the point 1.6 + 0.4i, then drag the blue dot again. Look at the shape of the blue area!

You may also notice the green dot jumping when the blue dot crosses the negative real axis. That is not a bug. It happens because some complex expressions have more than one valid answer. The computer chooses one value, and when it switches, the point appears to jump. That sudden change is called a discontinuity.

The reason there can be more than one answer is that angles wrap around. A rotation by 360 degrees brings you back to the same place. So if you are trying to undo a rotation, the original angle could differ by a full turn. In the complex world, that means expressions like exponentials and logarithms can have multiple values.

When you plot all the possible answers, they often line up along a logarithmic spiral.

That is why some operations stop being one-to-one functions when you move into complex numbers. Normally, a function gives one unique output for each input. In complex exponentiation and complex logarithms, a single input can lead to many outputs.

Terminology

Here are the key words used on this page, as well as others you might encounter while reading about complex numbers, explained in plain language.

Real Number

A number on the straight number line, such as 3, -2, 0.5, or -7.

Imaginary Number

A number that is a multiple of i. For example, 2i and -5i are imaginary numbers.

Complex Number

A number with both a real part and an imaginary part, written as z = a + bi.

Complex Plane

A flat space where the horizontal axis is the real part and the vertical axis is the imaginary part.

Magnitude (or Modulus or Absolute Value)

The distance of a complex number from the origin, found using r = √(a² + b²).

Argument (or Angle or Phase)

The direction the complex number points, measured from the positive real axis with θ = atan2(b, a).

Complex Conjugate

The number with the same real part but the opposite imaginary part: if z = a + bi, then z* = a - bi.

Conjugates are useful for dividing complex numbers and for simplifying expressions.

Conjugate Pair

A pair of complex numbers that are mirror images across the real axis, such as 3 + 4i and 3 - 4i.

Argand Diagram

Another name for the complex plane, where numbers are shown as points or arrows.

Cartesian (or Rectangular) Form

The standard way to write a complex number as z = a + bi.

Polar Form

The form z = r cis θ, where r is the magnitude and θ is the argument.

Exponential Form

The form z = reiθ, another compact way to write the same complex number.
